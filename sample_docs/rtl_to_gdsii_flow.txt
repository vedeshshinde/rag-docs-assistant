The RTL-to-GDSII Digital Design Flow

Building a digital chip starts from a functional description and ends with a manufacturing
file, and the sequence of steps in between is called the RTL-to-GDSII flow. RTL (register
transfer level) is a hardware description written in a language like Verilog or VHDL that
describes what the circuit should do in terms of registers and the logic between them.
GDSII is the final geometric file format handed to a semiconductor fab, describing the
exact shapes to be manufactured on each layer of the chip.

The flow generally proceeds through these stages. First, RTL design and verification:
engineers write RTL and confirm its functional correctness through simulation and, for
critical blocks, formal verification. Second, logic synthesis: a synthesis tool translates
the RTL into a gate-level netlist made of standard cells from a specific manufacturing
library, optimizing for area, power, and timing. Third, place and route: the netlist is
given physical locations and wire connections on the die, as described in the placement
and routing process. Fourth, static timing analysis and power analysis: the physical
design is checked against timing and power targets across all operating corners, often
requiring several iterations back into placement or synthesis to fix violations. Fifth,
physical verification: design rule checking (DRC) confirms the layout obeys the
manufacturing process's geometric rules, and layout-versus-schematic (LVS) checking
confirms the physical layout still matches the original netlist. Finally, tapeout: the
verified GDSII file is signed off and sent to the fab.

EDA (electronic design automation) teams build and maintain the internal tools, scripts,
and methodologies that automate and connect these stages, since commercial EDA vendor
tools (used for synthesis, P&R, STA, DRC/LVS) need process-specific configuration,
in-house scripting, and automation to run efficiently across a company's specific chip
designs and manufacturing nodes. A large part of this work involves data: parsing and
cleaning reports produced at each flow stage (timing reports, DRC violation reports,
power reports), tracking metrics across design iterations, and increasingly, applying
machine learning to predict problems (like timing violations or routing congestion)
earlier in the flow, before the expensive downstream stages are run.
