Standard Cell Placement and Routing

After a chip's logic has been synthesized into a netlist of standard cells (small,
pre-designed logic blocks like AND gates, flip-flops, and multiplexers), that netlist must
be physically arranged on the silicon die. This is done in two major stages: placement and
routing, together often called place-and-route (P&R).

Placement decides the physical x/y location of every standard cell on the chip. A good
placement minimizes total wirelength, reduces congestion in any one area, and keeps
timing-critical cells close together so signals do not have to travel far. Placement is
formulated as a large-scale optimization problem: with millions of cells, exhaustive
search is impossible, so tools use techniques like analytical placement (treating cells
and their connections as a system of springs to be minimized), followed by legalization
(snapping cells onto a manufacturable row-based grid) and detailed placement (local
swaps to fix remaining congestion or timing issues).

Routing then decides the exact metal wire paths that connect the pins of placed cells,
using the multiple metal layers available in the manufacturing process. Global routing
first assigns each connection (net) to a coarse region of the chip to balance congestion,
and detailed routing then computes exact wire geometry on each layer while obeying design
rules (minimum spacing, minimum width, via placement rules, etc). Both routing stages are
graph problems at their core: a net with more than two pins is modeled as a Steiner tree
problem on a grid graph, and detailed routing is frequently solved with maze-routing
algorithms that are variants of shortest-path search (similar in spirit to Dijkstra's
algorithm or A*) run over a grid representing available routing tracks.

Placement and routing quality directly affects whether the design will pass static timing
analysis, since wire length and layer choice both add delay to a signal's path. This is
why P&R and STA are run iteratively: a design is placed and routed, timed, and then
selectively re-placed or re-routed to fix any paths with negative slack, in a loop that
continues until the design closes timing across all corners.
